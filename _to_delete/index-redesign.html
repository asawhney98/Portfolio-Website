07 · Flagship case study Model-as-a-Service & AI Model-in-a-Box

Turning risk model execution from a 30-day project into a 7-day platform primitive.

An internal model-execution capability became the enterprise layer serving multiple business units — and model deployment stopped being bespoke work.

01Context

Risk models were everywhere. What ran them was not a platform.

Risk models sit inside the paths that matter most: opening an account, granting a line increase, checking spend power, authorising a transaction. Those decisions run across the US and 23 international markets, built by data science teams in multiple business units and legal entities. Execution had grown out of mainframe-based legacy platforms — it worked, but as a series of individual engineering projects rather than a capability teams could self-serve.

02Problem

Every model shipped like the first model.

Because each deployment was bespoke, the same work repeated every time: the same integration patterns, the same validation steps, the same test cycles. Roughly 60% of development effort was redundant, and metadata validation across model inputs was manual.

Before
  • 30 days from model ready to production
  • 6 test cycles per deployment
  • ~60% redundant development effort
  • Mainframe-based legacy execution
  • Manual metadata validation
→
After
  • 7 days to production — a 75%+ reduction
  • 2 test cycles per deployment
  • Standardised packaging removes the repeated build
  • Cloud-native microservices and REST APIs
  • Automated validation — +40% accuracy, −70% manual effort
03Why it mattered

Time-to-production is a business constraint, not an engineering one.

Thirty days to production is not a thirty-day cost. It is every model a data science team decides not to build because deployment is too expensive, and every risk strategy shipping against a stale model. Cutting the cycle to seven days means each model starts generating value 23 days earlier — worth $247K+ per model, per year. That figure multiplies by every model onboarded, which is the argument for building a platform at all.

04My role

Platform definition through launch.

  • Created the three-stage roadmap positioning MaaS as the enterprise-wide execution layer for risk models across business units.
  • Led prioritisation for a 13-member engineering team — 15+ features, 40+ new models.
  • Spearheaded AI Model-in-a-Box; conceptualised and launched the AI-powered Data Quality agent.
  • Drove onboarding of the Amex Singapore Data Science CoE and the automated SGP ⇄ US ML validation framework.
  • Led SDLC execution for the credit decisioning and model execution platforms.
05Product decisions

Three calls, and what each one cost.

  • Enterprise platform, not a business-unit service. Scoped for all risk model execution from the start. The cost: the sponsoring team waited on a general design instead of getting a bespoke one first.
  • Standardise the package, not the model. Model-in-a-Box constrains how a model is wrapped, validated and deployed — and deliberately does not touch modelling methodology. That boundary is what removed 60% redundancy without the platform dictating how data science works.
  • Three stages, not one cutover. Capability, then adoption, then cross-entity validation. Slower to full coverage, but each stage had to hold in a live decisioning path before the next one loaded onto it.
06Technical system

Why the architecture shape was a product decision.

Two properties drove the trade-offs. The platform serves real-time decisioning inside authorisation and account-opening paths and batch execution — so latency budgets and failure behaviour differ per consumer, not per model. And execution here is only useful if it is governed and auditable: a decision must be reconstructable afterwards. That is why data quality became its own product — metadata had to hold across the model, its real-time variables, and native data.

08What I learned
The principle

The real platform metric was never how many models we onboarded. It was how many teams stopped asking us for help.

A platform succeeds when it makes its own product team unnecessary. That changes what you prioritise: not the next integration, but the thing that lets the next integration happen without you.

AI Lab

Professional · American Express

Suspect Dispute Management

Problem
Dispute investigation ran on manual gathering plus batch ETL. Ops capacity was the ceiling, so attention skewed to higher-value cases.
Built
An agentic system replacing the ETL layer: the agent pulls data, calls an LLM for piece-wise call, chat and payment summaries, and assembles a report.
Approach
Agent orchestration in front of the LLM; summarisation split by source so each piece is separately checkable.
PM insight
Automate the assembly, not the judgement. The care professional still owns the decision — which is why throughput moved without shifting accountability.
Ed Gilligan Award
Personal · built and run solo

Autonomous GTM Infrastructure

Problem
LLM-generated outbound fails two ways at once: it hallucinates specifics, and it fails silently at dispatch. Most tooling solves neither.
Built
A reliability-first orchestration layer — grounded generation against a verified contact graph, then a stateful dispatch engine.
Approach
Eval layer, circuit breakers, per-recipient retry state machine, and a bounce-classification loop feeding back into targeting. Node.js, Groq, LLaMA 3.3-70b.
PM insight
Treat hallucination as a dispatch-blocking gate, not a quality score. Bad state gets isolated rather than propagated.
